Part two · the machines get connected

02 · Computer networks

You have used a network every day of your life and called it the internet, or the Wi-Fi, without needing to say what it is. A process on your machine now needs to reach a process on someone else's, and nothing from the last chapter can help: a memory address is a box number that means nothing on another machine, and neither machine can see the other's disk. Whatever crosses between them has to travel outside both, through something neither one owns. This chapter builds that something, one concession at a time.

Why you cannot just run a wire

Start with the obvious version. Run a wire between the two machines, agree that a high voltage means one and a low voltage means zero, and send the bytes. That works, and for two machines it is all you need. The trouble starts at three.

Three machines that all want to talk need three wires. Ten need forty five. A hundred need four thousand nine hundred and fifty, because every machine needs its own wire to every other one, which is n times n - 1, halved. The arithmetic is bad and the physical version is worse: your machine and mine are not in the same building, so a dedicated wire would have to be dug along every road between us, and then dug again for the next pair.

So machines have to share, which means some machines in the middle must accept traffic that is not for them and pass it along. Every question in this chapter follows from that one concession.

The telephone system had already solved this, wrongly

There was already a working global network for sharing links between many parties, and it was eighty years old. The telephone system built a path and held it: when you placed a call, the exchange found a chain of physical links from your phone to the other one and reserved every link for you, and nobody else could touch them. This is circuit switching, and for two people talking it is a good design, because the line is steady and the order of what you say is guaranteed by physics.

It suits computers badly, for two reasons. Computer conversations are mostly silence, so a reserved path sits unused for almost all of a session while nobody else is allowed near it. And the path is fixed at the start, so if any single link in the chain fails, the call ends. In the early 1960s that second one was the central objection rather than an inconvenience, because the people funding this work wanted communications that would survive parts of a country being destroyed.

Write the address on every piece

Two people arrived at the same answer separately, for those two different reasons. Paul Baran at RAND was solving survival, and Donald Davies at the National Physical Laboratory in England was solving the waste. Cut every message into small standard sized blocks and let each block find its own way across, reserving nothing. Davies gave the blocks the name that stuck: packets.

The move that makes it work is worth stating on its own. Write the destination address on every packet. Once each packet carries its own address, nothing has to be arranged in advance. A machine in the middle receives a packet, reads the address, decides which of its links points roughly the right way, passes it on, forgets about it, and handles the next one. A machine whose job is that is a router.

Because nothing is reserved, links are shared from moment to moment: when your conversation is silent, the capacity you are not using carries somebody else's packets without anyone arranging it. This is packet switching, and it is what the internet still runs on. The first network built this way was the ARPANET, funded by the American defence research agency, whose first two machines were connected in 1969.

What a network actually promises

Packet switching buys sharing and survival, and the cost is worth stating exactly, because the rest of this chapter is built to pay it back. Packets from one message can take different routes, so they can arrive in a different order from the one they left in. A router handed more traffic than it can forward throws packets away, so some do not arrive at all. And nothing is watching the message as a whole, because no machine in the middle knows a message exists.

Here is that, small enough to look at. Two routers, seven packets, each addressed to ada, and a router that can only hold four packets at once.

Read the outputs in order, because they are four different lessons. The first line is where loss comes from. gate did not fail and nothing was broken: seven packets arrived at a router that can hold four, so three were dropped on the floor. That is what congestion is, and it is the ordinary case rather than the exceptional one.

The second and third are what the receiving machine is handed and what it can do about it. Not a message, but a heap of fragments in an order nobody chose, which read as nonsense; sorting them by the number each one carries puts them back in the order they were written, without anyone in the middle having tracked anything.

The fourth cannot be fixed by sorting. Packets 1, 2 and 3 are not late, they are gone, and the sorted text has a hole where they should be. But look at what the receiver knows: it holds 0, 4, 5 and 6, and it can name exactly which numbers are missing, because the numbering told it what to expect. Knowing something is missing is not the same as having it, and it is what you need before you can ask for it again.

Notice also what the routers never had. Neither knew what the message said, that six other packets existed, or that a conversation was happening. Each read one address, picked one link, and forgot. That ignorance is not a simplification for the sake of the example, it is the design.

The two things a network costs

Sharing is not the only price. A network charges you in two currencies, and confusing them is behind a great deal of bad engineering. Bandwidth is how many bytes per second you can push, and you can buy more of it. Latency is how long one packet takes to get there, and you cannot, because a packet cannot outrun light through fibre and it stops at every router on the way to be read and forwarded.

Eleven milliseconds to a machine in the same country, a hundred and seventy six to one in Japan and back. No amount of money changes the second number much, because most of it is distance. That is why a page that makes thirty small requests one after another feels slow no matter how fast the server is, and why so much of what the later chapters build is an attempt to make fewer round trips or to make them from somewhere closer.

Who fixes it, and where

By the early 1970s there was more than one network, each with its own rules, its own maximum packet size and its own ideas about reliability, and they could not talk to each other. Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn published the answer in 1974, and its central move is a refusal: assume nothing about the networks underneath. Put a machine at the join between two of them to pass packets across, give every machine an address in one shared scheme that spans all of them, and let each network carry packets however it likes internally. That gives a network of networks, which is where the name internet comes from. Notice what that shared address does not do: it names a machine, not one of the dozens of programs running on it, and deciding which program a packet is meant for needs a second number that this design has not got.

The middle stays ignorant, exactly as the two routers above were. Everything about ordering, loss and retransmission is dealt with only by the two machines at the ends. This is the end to end principle, and it is why the middle of the internet has never had to be upgraded to understand the web, or video calls, or anything invented after it was built. The middle does not understand any of them. It moves packets.

The design was then split in two, because not every program wants the same promises. IP, the Internet Protocol, is the modest half: it carries one packet towards an address, may lose it, deliver it twice, or deliver it late, and it runs everywhere, including on every router in the middle. TCP, the Transmission Control Protocol, is the demanding half, and it runs only on the two machines at the ends. It numbers the bytes it sends, the receiver sends back short notes saying how far it has got, anything not acknowledged is sent again, and anything arriving out of order is held until the gap in front of it is filled. What comes out the far side is an ordered, complete stream of bytes.

That is the listing you just ran, made automatic and continuous. The sorting is the sequence numbers. Noticing that packets 1, 2 and 3 never arrived is what triggers the request to send them again, which is the second exercise below.

These pieces stack, and the stacking is the point. The bottom layer, Ethernet or Wi-Fi, moves a chunk of bytes to the next machine on the same local link. IP uses that to move a packet across many joined networks to an address. TCP uses that to give two programs an ordered, reliable stream. The payoff is replacement: Wi-Fi did not exist when IP was designed, and adding it changed nothing above the bottom layer.

Check it yourself: count the machines in the middle

The claim that your packets pass through machines run by strangers is easy to check. traceroute sends packets with a deliberately short lifespan: each carries a count of how many machines it may pass through, every machine that forwards it lowers that count by one, and the one that lowers it to zero throws the packet away and sends back a complaint. Send a packet allowed one hop, then one allowed two, and the complaints name the machines along the route in order.

There is no wire from this machine to that one. There are six machines in between, owned by at least three different companies, none of which has any relationship with the author or with the site being reached. Each accepted a packet that was not addressed to it and passed it on, which is the concession from the start of this chapter, made concrete.

if you see stars, or a different route

Runs often contain lines of * * * where a machine declined to send its complaint back. Packets still pass through it perfectly well. Run it twice and the route may change, because nothing was reserved, so nothing is obliged to stay the same.

Exercises

  1. predict

    In packets.py, change HOLD from 4 to 2, so each router can hold two packets at once. Write down how many of the seven arrive, and which numbers, before running it.

    Two arrive. Halving what a router can hold did not halve the loss, it took it from three packets to five, because the packets that survive one router are the only ones that can reach the next. Small changes in capacity at one point produce large changes in what gets through, which is why congestion collapses rather than degrades.

  2. build

    The receiver can see which numbers are missing. Use that. Write a loop that sends the message, works out what did not arrive, sends only those again, and repeats until it holds everything, counting the rounds. Print the reassembled message at the end.

    Two rounds, and the message comes out whole. You have just written retransmission, which is most of what TCP does on your behalf. Notice what it needed: numbers on the packets and a receiver that reports back. Notice also what it did not need: any change to the routers.

  3. break

    Real networks do not only lose packets, they sometimes deliver the same one twice, because a router forwarded it down two links or a retransmission crossed a late arrival. Change the network so that each packet has roughly a two in five chance of arriving twice, lose nothing at all, and reassemble with the same sorting as before.

    Every packet arrives, the check for gaps reports nothing missing, and the message is still wrong: a networa network does not delivot deliver.... Sorting is not enough. The receiver has to keep each number once and throw away the second copy, which is why TCP's sequence numbers do double duty, both ordering the stream and telling it what it has already seen.

in the wild

That 172.66.147.243 belongs to Cloudflare, a company that sits in front of a large part of the web, which chapter six meets by reading its headers. When a video call breaks up, you are watching packets.py: the sender did not stop, a router in the middle ran out of room. And the layering pays off in your browser right now. QUIC, the protocol under HTTP/3, replaces TCP with something built on the modest half instead, and it could be deployed at all only because the middle of the network never understood TCP in the first place.

Where this runs out

where this runs out

A process on this machine can now hand bytes to a process on a machine somewhere else, in order and without losses, without either of them owning any of the wire in between. What neither has is any idea what the bytes mean. The network moves numbers and has no opinion about whether a run of 4,206 bytes is a photograph, a price, or a login attempt. TCP delivers them faithfully, and the receiving program is then holding 4,206 bytes and has to decide on its own what it has been given. Two programs that disagree about what the bytes mean will both work perfectly and understand nothing.

So a network on its own is not useful. What is useful is the agreements people build on top of it about what the bytes mean, and there have been many: mail, file transfer, news, chat.

The web was one of these agreements, it arrived late, and there was no obvious reason for it to beat the ones already established. Why it did is the next chapter.
