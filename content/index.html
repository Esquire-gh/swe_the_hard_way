Software Engineering the Hard Way

If you are here, you have probably heard of software engineering, or programming, or coding, and you have probably tried some of it. You may have written a fair amount of Python, followed a few tutorials through to a todo app or a clone of something famous, and still found that when you try to build something of your own, something a little larger, it does not come together. That is not a lack of talent. It is that nobody showed you what a program is made of, and without that there is nothing for your intuition to stand on.

This course is software engineering from the ground up. It teaches the fundamentals: the pieces every program is made of, why they are shaped the way they are, and the problem each one was invented to solve. With those in hand you can form your own intuitions, turn an idea into a design and the design into code, and recognise what is going on inside the higher-level tools you meet later, because you will have built a small version of each one yourself.

Why the hard way

It is called the hard way because it does the things a tutorial lets you skip. A tutorial hands you a framework and a folder of files and has you fill in the blanks, and you end up able to ship something and unable to say what any of it does. Here you meet each problem before you meet the tool that solves it, so that when the tool arrives it feels like relief instead of another thing to memorise. By the end you will have written a web server in a single file, broken it on purpose, and understood every framework you ever pick up as a set of answers to problems you felt in your own hands.

What kind of software this builds

The words software engineering cover an enormous range, and most of it is not the web. The app on your phone, the operating system underneath it, the firmware in a microwave or a car, a video game, a spreadsheet on the desktop, a data pipeline that runs overnight: each is software, and each is shaped by the device it runs on and the way it runs.

This course builds one of those kinds in depth, web software: programs that run on a server somewhere and answer requests arriving over a network. It is the kind most people are drawn to first, it is the kind whose whole stack you can hold in your head and build end to end, and the ideas you meet building it, processes, protocols, storage, doing more than one thing at once, are the same ideas underneath the phone app and the game and the operating system. Learn them here and the rest have ground to stand on.

It ends with AI, on purpose. The systems everyone is suddenly talking about are, at their core, software engineering: a process that reads a great deal of data, a server that answers requests, the same pieces you will have built by then, arranged a new way. When you get there it should look familiar.

Who this is for

Anyone who can write a little Python. No computer science background is assumed. If you already have one, the early chapters will move fast and the later ones will still have something for you, because the point is never the facts on their own but the way they connect.

The journey

Five parts, eighteen chapters. It starts on a single computer, with a program that has not yet run, and ends with the systems that serve billions of people, the AI systems among them. Nothing in between is skipped, and nothing is called magic. Each chapter opens with the exact question the last one left unanswered, so the whole thing reads as a single line of reasoning rather than a pile of topics.

meaning is an agreement; everything else is a cost.

That sentence turns out to be the whole course. A byte means a letter because we agreed it would. A request is a request because both sides agreed on the shape of the text. Every layer is one more agreement, and every agreement is paid for in time, memory, or the risk of getting it wrong.

Running the code

Every chapter that shows code ships that code as a real file you can run, under code/, one folder per chapter. The output printed in the text is the actual output of the actual file. Where a number depends on your machine, the text says so, and what it claims is the shape of the result, not the digits.

Chapters one through twelve and fourteen through eighteen need only Python three and its standard library. Chapter thirteen installs one framework, and only after you have felt why it needs to exist. The servers run until you stop them with control C. Everything here was written and run on macOS and Linux; on Windows, use WSL.

The machine these numbers came from

Every measurement on this site was produced by running the code on one laptop, an Apple silicon MacBook reporting eighteen processors. Your absolute numbers will be different, and that is expected. What the site claims are the ratios and the shapes, one strategy against another, and those hold across machines.
