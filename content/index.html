from the ground up

Software Engineering the Hard Way

This course teaches web based software engineering from the ground up, and helps you build your own intuition as you grow as an engineer.

It teaches the fundamentals: the pieces every program is made of, why they are shaped the way they are, and the problem each one was invented to solve. With those in hand you can turn an idea into a design and the design into code, and recognise what is going on inside the higher level tools you meet later, because you will have built a small version of each one yourself.

Why the hard way

It is called the hard way because it does what a course usually lets you skip. Most hand you a framework and a folder of files to fill in, and you end up able to ship something and unable to say what any of it does. Here you meet each problem before the tool that solves it: you write the accept loop before anybody says a framework has one, lose data to a file before anybody says database, and watch two visitors collide before you hear the word thread. By the end, FastAPI, nginx, Postgres and Docker are not things to memorise. They are answers to problems you felt in your own hands.

What kind of software this builds

Software engineering covers an enormous range, and most of it is not the web. The app on your phone, the operating system underneath it, the firmware in a car, a video game: each is shaped by the device it runs on. This course builds one kind in depth, web software, meaning programs that run on a machine somewhere and answer requests arriving over a network. It is the kind whose whole stack you can hold in your head and build end to end, and its ideas are the same ideas underneath the phone app and the game. It ends with AI on purpose, because those systems are a process reading data and a server answering requests, which by then you will have built twice.

Who this is for

Anyone who can write a little Python. No computer science background is assumed. If you already have one, the early chapters will move fast and the later ones will still have something for you, because the point is never the facts on their own but the way they connect.

How a chapter works

Every chapter opens with the question the last one left unanswered, then builds something small enough to type, then breaks it. Five things recur, and they always mean the same thing.

  • A puzzle appears where two facts collide. It asks a question and stops. The page answers it by running something, not by telling you in the next sentence.
  • Check it yourself is a short observation that proves the chapter was telling the truth, with the output you should get.
  • Three exercises close every chapter. Predict what a run will print before you run it, build the thing the chapter said was missing, then break it on purpose. Each says what you should see. None gives you the answer.
  • In the wild names the real tool that does what you just built by hand, and the exact line in it.
  • Where this runs out is the limit you just hit. It is the next chapter's first paragraph.

The journey

Eighteen chapters and a closing walk. It starts on a single computer, with a program that has not yet run, and ends with the systems that serve billions of people. Each chapter opens on the exact question the last one left, so the whole thing reads as a single line of reasoning rather than a pile of topics.

meaning is an agreement; everything else is a cost.

Nothing inside a computer means anything on its own. Two bytes are the word hi because a committee agreed in 1963 that they would be, and the same two bytes are the number 26,729 if you read them the other way.

Every layer in this course is one more agreement of that kind, and every agreement is paid for in time, in memory, or in the risk of getting it wrong.

What you need

A laptop, a terminal, and Python 3. Everything else below is already on macOS and on most Linux machines, and each is introduced where it is first needed. Nothing gets installed until a chapter has argued for it, which happens exactly once, in chapter sixteen.

the tools this course uses

python3 throughout. traceroute in chapter two, dig in four, curl in six, nc in seven, ps in eight, lsof in nine and twelve. The commands are written for macOS and Linux. On Windows, use WSL.

Running the code

Every chapter that shows code ships it as a real file under code/, one folder per chapter, and the output printed on the page is that file's actual output. Some of the programs are servers: they print the address they are listening on and run until you stop them with control C, and the page says what to do from a second terminal.

Every measurement here came from one laptop, an Apple silicon MacBook reporting eighteen processors. Your numbers will differ. What the text claims is the shape of a result and the ratio between two of them, never the digits.
