Part five · more users, more machines

17 · Introduction to distributed systems

Everything so far, the server, the database, the cache, the queue, the proxy, has been a process on one computer. One computer has a ceiling, and past it the system spreads across several, which is what the words distributed system mean. Systems spread for three different reasons, and it helps to keep them apart. All three put the network from chapter two between parts that used to share a machine, and that is where a lot of writing about software becomes vague. The change is precise, and this chapter is the list of things that stop being true.

Three reasons to spread

A system whose parts run on more than one machine and talk over a network is a distributed system, and the reason to have one is never that it is nicer. It is that something no longer fits. The first thing to stop fitting is usually load. One copy of your application, even behind chapter fifteen's proxy with a thread pool, can answer only so many requests a second, so the move is to run the same program on several machines and have the proxy spread requests across them. Adding machines rather than buying a bigger one is called horizontal scaling, and it is the reason the proxy existed in the first place.

It is worth running once rather than taking on faith. horizontal.py starts two copies of a work-doing server as separate processes, so each gets its own core and its own interpreter lock, puts chapter twelve's proxy in front spreading requests across them, and measures the rate through one copy and then through both.

Twice the machines, twice the work, because the ceiling was one core and there are now two. That the copies are separate processes matters: two threads in one process would have shared the lock and bought nothing, which is the trap chapter thirteen described from the other side.

The second thing to stop fitting is the data. It can be copied to several machines so that it survives one of them dying and so that more readers can be served at once, which is replication. Or it can be split across machines according to a key, so that the whole of it no longer has to fit on one disk, which is sharding. The third is capability. The database on its own machine with its own memory, the cache on another, the queue on a third, each doing one job with the hardware that job wants. That split is the reason chapter fourteen said a real database has a server in front of it: eight application machines cannot open one file, but they can all connect to one program.

The change is precise. On one machine, two processes share a clock, a disk, and memory, and when one asks the other for something either the answer comes back or the whole machine is gone. Across machines none of that holds: everything travels over chapter two's network, which reorders packets, loses some, and never says how long it will take. Specific things that were reliably true stop being true, and the rest of the chapter is those things, worst first.

The thing that becomes impossible

the experiment

Your program sends a request to another machine and no answer comes back within the time you were willing to wait. Here is the complete list of what may have happened. The request never arrived. It arrived and the machine failed before doing anything. It did the work and then failed. It did the work and the reply was lost on the way back. Nothing failed at all and the machine is slow.

Which of those happened? Find a message you could send, or a check you could perform, that tells them apart.

From where you are standing those five are identical. There is no message you can send, no check you can perform, and no cleverness you can apply that separates them, because every one of those checks is another request that can fail in the same five ways. no_answer.py runs a slow but perfectly healthy server, and a client that waits half a second.

The card was charged. The client has no evidence of it and never will. Nothing in either program is broken, and no amount of care in either of them would change the outcome, because the uncertainty is not in the code. It is in the arrangement. Almost everything else in this chapter is a way of living with that sentence.

Retries, and why chapter fifteen's word matters everywhere

Since you cannot find out what happened, the only move available is to ask again. And asking again may cause the work to be done twice. Chapter fifteen introduced idempotent as a property that jobs on a queue need. It is not a queue detail. It is the rule for anything that crosses a machine boundary, because anything that crosses one may be sent twice by a client that was never told the first attempt worked.

Getting it is a matter of how you say things. "Add five to the balance" is wrong twice. "Set the balance to thirty" is right twice. "Apply transfer 8f2c" is right any number of times if the far side keeps a note of the transfers it has already applied, and the client rather than the server has to choose that identifier, because the client is the only one who knows that the second attempt is a repeat of the first. The timeout itself is a choice with no correct answer. Too short and you abandon work that succeeded and do it again. Too long and you hold threads from chapter thirteen waiting on a machine that is already gone, which is the beginning of a failure described further down. What you can do is choose the number against a measurement rather than a feeling, which is what chapter fifteen's p99 was for.

Nobody agrees what time it is

The natural way to settle which of two writes is newer is to compare timestamps. It does not work, and the way it fails is quiet. clocks.py has one machine's clock 120 milliseconds ahead of another's, which is an ordinary amount, and the offset is written down plainly at the top of the file.

Machines whose clocks are kept in step are kept in step to within some error, never exactly, and a machine that has lost touch with its time source drifts. So whenever the difference between two clocks is larger than the gap between two writes, keeping the later timestamp keeps the older write. That is data being discarded with no error reported anywhere, by code that looks correct. The same program shows the other half of it.

The wall clock is a number somebody may change, including backwards, which is why every measurement in this course used time.monotonic or perf_counter instead. The way out is to stop asking when something happened and start asking what it knew. If every write carries a record of the writes it had already seen, then the system can tell the difference between one write that came after another and two writes made by parties who had not heard of each other. Counters used that way are called logical clocks. They do not remove the problem, they name it: the second case is a genuine conflict, and something or somebody has to decide what the answer is.

Copies, and a choice you cannot avoid

Replication keeps the same data on more than one machine, and once there is more than one copy, a write arrives at one of them and a read may be served by another that has not caught up yet. Twenty lines are enough to watch it happen.

The copy answered 0 for a balance that was already 100, and nothing was broken; the write was still in flight. There are exactly two things you can do about it. The write is not finished until every copy has it. Then every read is correct, writes take as long as the slowest copy, and if one copy is unreachable you cannot write at all. Or the write is finished when one copy has it and spreads afterwards. Then writes are fast and keep working when a machine is missing, and some reader somewhere gets an answer that is out of date. Neither is the wrong answer. They are the two answers to a question that has no free option.

The sharpest version happens when the network splits the machines into two groups that cannot reach each other, which is called a partition and which does happen. Each side now knows it cannot see the others and must choose: refuse to answer, and stay correct, or answer, and risk being wrong. That is the whole content of the result people call CAP. It is a statement about what you must give up during a partition, not a menu of three properties from which you select two. A system where the copies agree once writing stops and enough time passes is described as eventually consistent. Whether that is acceptable depends entirely on how long eventually is and on what somebody might do in the meantime, which is a question about your users rather than about databases.

Splitting instead of copying, and needing one answer anyway

Copies help reading and help survival. They do nothing for writing or for size, because every machine still holds everything and every write still goes everywhere. So the other move is sharding, sending some records to one machine and some to another according to a key. The word partitioning means this same splitting of data, and is worth keeping apart from the partition above, which was the network splitting into groups that cannot reach each other; one word, two unrelated meanings. It buys write capacity and lets the data be larger than any single machine, and it costs in two ways worth knowing in advance. Any question that spans shards has to be asked of several machines and the answers combined, so questions that were one line become a small distributed program of their own. And the key has to spread the load, because a key that puts your busiest customers on one machine has given you all the complexity and none of the capacity.

Some decisions cannot be eventually consistent. Which machine is currently in charge. Whether this seat has been sold. For these there has to be exactly one answer and it has to be agreed on. The method is voting. A group of machines accepts a decision when more than half of them have accepted it, and the reason it is a majority rather than a number is worth seeing. Two groups cannot both contain more than half the machines, so a split network cannot produce two conflicting decisions. This is consensus, and the two well known descriptions of how to do it are Paxos and Raft. The price is exactly what it sounds like. Nothing can be decided unless more than half the machines are reachable, and every decision costs at least one trip across the network. Which is why systems use consensus for the small important things, such as who is in charge and what the configuration is, and avoid it for every write.

When slow is worse than dead

Come back to the first section, because it has a consequence that surprises people who have only run one machine. A machine that has died is easy. Connections to it are refused immediately, you find out in a millisecond, and you can do something else. A machine that is still answering, slowly, is the dangerous one. Your server calls another service that has become slow. Its threads, in the sense of chapter thirteen, fill up with requests that are waiting rather than working. Since there is a fixed number of them, your server becomes slow. Whatever calls your server now fills up in the same way. One slow component takes down everything in front of it, and none of those things are broken. Retries make it worse rather than better. A service that is struggling starts receiving more traffic than it did when it was healthy, because everybody who gave up is asking again.

The fast page does no slow work and still took nearly two seconds, because all four workers were waiting on the slow service and there was none left to run it. That is the whole cascade in one process: the slowness of a machine you do not control becomes the slowness of yours, through the fixed pool from chapter thirteen, and then of whatever calls you.

The answers all have the shape of giving up on purpose. Have a worse answer ready and use it, such as a cached page or a sensible default. Stop sending requests to something that keeps failing, for a while, so it can recover, which is a circuit breaker. Decide in advance how many requests you will hold at once and refuse the rest immediately rather than accepting them and never answering, which is backpressure, and a fast refusal is kinder to everybody than a connection that hangs. And when retrying, wait longer each time and add a random amount, because a thousand clients that all retry after exactly one second arrive as one spike.

Check it yourself: give the client more patience

Change client_socket.settimeout(0.5) to client_socket.settimeout(3.0) in no_answer.py and run it again.

terminal
$ python3 no_answer.py
the client got: HTTP/1.1 200 OK
what the client can prove happened: ['/charge-the-card']
what actually happened:             ['/charge-the-card']

Nothing else changed. The same server did the same work and took the same one and a half seconds. The only difference between a failed request and a successful one was a number chosen by the client. That is worth sitting with, because it does not happen inside one process. There, whether a function returned is a fact about the world. Across machines, whether a request succeeded is a judgement your client makes from incomplete information, using a threshold you picked, and it can be wrong in both directions.

Exercises

  1. predict

    A client charges a card, times out, and retries, up to three attempts, against a server that is slow but healthy and charges on every request it receives. Predict how many times the card is charged before you run retry_charges.py.

    Three times. Every attempt reached the server and did its work; the client gave up each time before the reply arrived and, having no way to know the charge happened, asked again. This is the impossible-to-tell problem from the top of the chapter turned into money: the retry is not a bug, it is the only move available, and it is exactly what makes the double charge. The customer's anger is the system working as designed.

  2. build

    Make the retry safe. Have the client put the same Idempotency-Key on every attempt of one charge, and have the server keep a set of the keys it has already applied and skip the work when it sees one again. Keep retrying until the charge lands exactly once.

    idempotency_key.py charges once across three attempts. The two halves both matter: the client chooses the key, because only the client knows the three attempts are one charge, and the server keeps the set, because only the server can refuse to do the work twice. This is the whole of what "make it idempotent" means in practice, and it is a few lines, but notice the set lives in memory and dies with the process, so a real one keeps those keys in the database from chapter fourteen, which is where every hard part in this course eventually goes.

  3. break

    Take no_answer.py and move the server's sleep to sit between charging the card and sending the reply, then run it. Compare what the client believes with what the server's ledger records, and decide which one is right.

    whose_ledger.py runs both orderings, and in both the client times out believing nothing happened while the server's ledger says the charge went through. The server's ledger is right, always, because the charge is a fact on the server and the client's belief is a guess from a timeout. This is the mental shift the chapter is really about: inside one process, whether a function ran is a fact; across machines, whether a request succeeded is an opinion the client holds with incomplete evidence, and the two can disagree in both directions.

in the wild

Each idea here is a product or a protocol. Stripe's Idempotency-Key header is exactly the build exercise: you send it with a charge, and a retry with the same key never charges twice. NTP is the clock section's losing battle, keeping machines in step to within some error and never exactly. Read replicas are the copies, and their lag is why an app sometimes shows you a comment that vanishes on refresh. etcd and ZooKeeper are consensus as a service, used for the small important decisions, who is in charge and what the configuration is, and nothing else. And 429 Too Many Requests with a Retry-After header is backpressure written into HTTP: a server refusing you fast, on purpose, and telling you when to come back, which is kinder than a connection that hangs.

The part that gets left out

That is the shape of the subject. Nothing can be known for certain, clocks disagree, copies force a choice between being right and being available, agreement costs a majority and a round trip, and slow is worse than dead.

where this runs out

Most treatments stop about here. The trouble is that the loudest part of the industry sits just past that line and is written about as if it had its own physics. It does not. A model being trained is a process reading files off a disk, given turns by an operating system, spread across machines with the coordination problems in this chapter; a model answering questions is a server on a socket, behind a queue and a cache.

The last chapter walks the whole stack again with those names attached, and its point is not to teach you anything new.
